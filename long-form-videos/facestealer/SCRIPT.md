# The Fake Video Call That Stole $400 Million

## 1. Cold open

You join a video call with someone you trust. Their face is on screen. Their room
looks familiar. They know why you are there. Then the audio breaks.

A message appears: your microphone needs a quick update. The person on screen
waits while you click the fix.

But that person is not on the call. You are watching a recording stolen from an
earlier victim. The repair button is a trap. And before the call ends, your own
face may be recorded and prepared for the next target.

This is FaceStealer, a social-engineering campaign the FBI says stole more than
four hundred million dollars in eighteen months. Investigators seized more than
nine hundred webcam videos belonging to people working in cryptocurrency and
Web three.

The frightening part is not a futuristic artificial intelligence breaking
encryption. It is a familiar face removing the moment of doubt that normally
stops a scam.

## 2. The evidence

On November twelfth, twenty twenty-six, the United States Justice Department
announced that it had disrupted infrastructure used in the campaign. The FBI
published the mechanism in unusually direct language.

The attackers used imitation versions of popular video-conferencing services.
They secretly recorded webcam video, compromised communication accounts, and
then contacted people who already trusted the victim.

That detail changes the attack. A random stranger asking you to install software
is suspicious. A colleague, founder, developer, or investor you already know is
different. The message arrives through a real compromised account. The meeting
appears to contain a real face. The conversation is built around work the target
already expects to discuss.

The campaign has been tracked under names connected to North Korean cyber
operations, including Sapphire Sleet, APT thirty-eight, BlueNoroff, and
TraderTraitor. Naming conventions differ between agencies and security firms.
The behavior is what matters.

This was not one fake celebrity advertisement sent to millions of strangers.
It was a chain. Each successful compromise created the material and access
needed to approach the next person.

## 3. The victim loop

The loop begins before the target knows there is a scam.

First, the attacker gets webcam footage. It may come from an earlier fake
meeting, a compromised system, or another intrusion. The FBI says investigators
identified and seized more than nine hundred such recordings.

Second, the attacker compromises a communication account. Now the invitation
does not come from a newly created profile with no history. It can come from an
account the next target already recognizes.

Third, the attacker sends a normal-looking work message. The subject might be
a project, an investment, a code review, or a meeting the target has a reason
to accept.

Fourth, the target enters an imitation meeting page. The stolen video creates
the impression that the trusted contact is present. Recorded footage can be
replayed, cut, or combined with synthetic media. The victim sees enough human
behavior to stop checking the small details.

Fifth, something conveniently fails. Audio will not connect. The camera needs
permission. A browser component appears to be missing. The page offers a fix.
The FBI warns that these fake audio-repair links can install malware.

Sixth, the attackers use the new access to steal credentials, reach financial
systems, or move deeper into a company. They also gain a fresh identity, a fresh
account, and potentially fresh webcam footage.

Then the process starts again.

That is why the name FaceStealer fits. The stolen asset is not only a password.
It is the victim's credibility.

## 4. Why the call works

People often imagine deepfake fraud as a perfect synthetic person speaking in
real time. The attack does not need to be perfect. It only needs to survive the
few seconds in which the target decides whether the call feels normal.

Video calls already contain delay, compression, frozen frames, bad lighting,
muted microphones, and people speaking over each other. The defects that might
expose manipulated media can look like ordinary meeting problems.

The attackers also control the setting. If the audio is broken, the fake person
does not need to answer a difficult spontaneous question. If the meeting is
short, the recording needs to remain convincing for only a short time. If the
message came from a compromised account, the target may explain away the rest.

Artificial intelligence can make this faster by cleaning recordings, changing
voices, generating plausible messages, or producing variations for different
targets. But the decisive technology is social context. The attackers borrow
trust that already exists between two real people.

This also explains why open-source maintainers, crypto employees, and Web three
teams are attractive targets. A single developer or operator may have access to
code, signing systems, wallets, infrastructure, and other people with even more
access.

## 5. The Binance comparison

The same Justice Department announcement included another enormous figure.
More than six billion dollars in virtual assets had been stolen from Binance in
late October. The Associated Press reported an estimate of about six point three
billion dollars, making it the largest cryptocurrency heist on record. American
authorities said more than one point two billion dollars had been seized or
restrained.

That story is already attracting millions of views, and it creates an easy but
incorrect headline: artificial intelligence hackers used deepfakes to steal six
billion dollars from Binance.

The public evidence does not establish that. The Justice Department describes
the Binance theft as separate from FaceStealer and attributes it to North Korean
military actors tracked as TraderTraitor. FaceStealer is the campaign involving
imitation meetings, recorded webcam footage, and impersonation. Binance is a
separate cyber theft reported in the same enforcement announcement.

The connection is scale, timing, and the state-backed focus on cryptocurrency.
It is not proof that one technique caused both crimes.

This distinction matters because a dramatic false claim can make a strong video
for one day and destroy the channel's credibility afterward.

## 6. The wider pattern

The Nvidia phishing campaign reported this week shows how quickly criminals can
attach themselves to breaking news. Emails offered supposed access to Nvidia
investment information through an executive-partnership invitation. The lure
was tailored to people likely to care about the company's leadership crisis and
stock volatility.

The losses and identity of the attacker were not publicly established when this
video was made. It belongs here as a warning about speed, not as another proven
four-hundred-million-dollar case.

The pattern is consistent. Criminals do not wait for a story to settle. They use
the period when people are urgently searching for answers, documents, access,
or insider context. Generative tools reduce the time needed to write convincing
messages and adapt them to a specific industry.

FaceStealer adds something more dangerous: the breaking-news lure can arrive
through the face and account of someone you already know.

## 7. What actually stops it

Four habits interrupt this attack without requiring anyone to become a deepfake
expert.

First, verify the meeting through a second channel. Call the person using a
number or account you already had. Do not use the contact information supplied
inside the suspicious invitation.

Second, never install a codec, microphone repair, browser update, or meeting
component because a call page tells you to. Close the page and open the official
application yourself.

Third, check the complete domain, not the logo or the first familiar word in the
address. Imitation meeting pages are designed to look right for the few seconds
before a click.

Fourth, protect communication accounts as seriously as financial accounts. Use
hardware-backed security keys where possible, review active sessions, and remove
unknown devices. A compromised chat account can be the bridge to every person
who trusts its owner.

For companies holding cryptocurrency, the check must continue beyond the call.
No single meeting, message, or familiar face should be enough to approve a
high-value change. Independent confirmation and separation of duties create the
delay an attacker is trying to remove.

The next fake call may not look fake. That is the point. Trust the person, but
verify the channel before the face on screen becomes yours.
