## S1 · Seventy-two  (≈1:10)
*[Dawn. A doorstep. A rice-flour line draws itself into a 3×3 grid. Numbers appear one by one.]*
- Every morning, at doorsteps across South India, someone bends down and draws nine numbers in rice flour.
- Add any line. Across. Down. Corner to corner. Seventy-two. Every single time.
- This is the Kubera Yantra, the seat of the god who guards the treasures of the three worlds.
- No ancient book writes these numbers down. They have lived in people's hands, generation after generation.
- And inside them, something is hidden. Take nineteen away from every number…
*[27→8, 20→1 … the grid flips: the Lo Shu on a turtle shell.]*
- …and a square appears that China says rose from a river on the back of a turtle.
- Tonight we decode the Kubera Yantra the way the tradition itself reads it. The god. The square. The mantra. The rules. And the logic of how it is said to draw wealth, and keep it.
*[Title: THE KUBERA CODE · Hidden in Plain Sight]*

## S2 · The god of what is hidden  (AV 8.10.28; ŚB 13.4.3.10; TA 1.31; Śiva Purāṇa)  ≈2:10
*[Starfield. The cosmic cow in gold line; a calf beside her.]*
- Start with the god. The oldest Kubera is not the one you think.
- In the Atharvaveda, the cosmic cow goes to the hidden folk, and they milk a single power from her.

> **CHANT** (AV 8.10.28): तस्याः कुबेरो वैश्रवणो वत्स आसीदामपात्रं पात्रम् । तां रजतनाभिः कबेरकोऽधोक्तां तिरोधामेवाधोक् ॥

- Her calf was Kubera Vaiśravaṇa. And what they milked from her was concealment.
- Now listen to the word for treasure: nidhi. Ni-dhā, to put away. Concealment is tiro-dhā, to put out of sight. The same root sits in both. In the Veda's own language, treasure is wealth that has been put away.
- The Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa names his people: the Rakṣas… and, in the assembly, robbers.
- And the Śiva Purāṇa goes further. It says Kubera once was a thief.
*[Night. A Śiva temple. A young man creeps in; a lamp gutters.]*
- Guṇanidhi, a Brahmin's son, gambled everything away. One Śivarātri night he crept into a Śiva temple to steal the offerings. It was dark, so he tore his own cloth into a wick and lit the lamp.
- He was caught and killed. But Śiva's attendants took him from Yama's messengers, because of that one lamp, and that one night of fasting.
- Lifetimes later, the thief became Kubera, lord of all treasure.
- The Taittirīya Āraṇyaka gives him a whole Vedic rite. It opens: concealment was born from the waters. And before anything is asked, the first offering goes to a strange guest: your obstructing brother, who lives in my heart, so that he does not destroy my aims.
- Remember this brother. He comes back at the end.

## S3 · The guardian who asked for nothing  (Rām. 7.3, 7.11, 7.13, 7.15; Amarakośa; Bharhut)  ≈2:10
*[An ascetic; years pass as rings of light. Brahmā on a lotus.]*
- Vālmīki tells how this god rose. Vaiśravaṇa, son of the sage Viśravas, sits in tapas for thousands of years: living on water, then on air, then on nothing at all. Brahmā appears and offers a boon.

> **CHANT** (Rāmāyaṇa 7.3.14): अथाब्रवीद्वैश्रवणः पितामहमुपस्थितम् । भगवँल्लोकपालत्वमिच्छेयं वित्तरक्षणम् ॥

- "Lord, I wish to be a guardian of the world… and the protector of wealth."
- Not to own it. To guard it.
- Brahmā makes him the fourth guardian, beside Indra, Yama and Varuṇa, and gives him the flying chariot, Puṣpaka.
*[Laṅkā in gold; a shadow crosses it.]*
- He rules Laṅkā, until his half-brother Rāvaṇa demands it. Their father tells Kubera: leave, go to Kailāsa. Kubera obeys. Later Rāvaṇa takes the Puṣpaka too.
- On Kailāsa, Kubera's left eye falls on Pārvatī, and her radiance scorches it yellow. Śiva, pleased with his eight-hundred-year vow, makes him a friend. From then on he is Ekākṣipiṅgala: the one with a single yellow eye.
*[REAL: the Bharhut relief inscribed "Kupiro Yakho".]*
- Two thousand one hundred years ago, sculptors at the Bharhut stūpa carved him as a guardian of the north, and wrote his name beside him: Kupiro Yakho, Kubera the Yakṣa. Even the Buddhist canon names his northern city: Āḷakamandā, the Alakā of the Purāṇas.
- The Amarakośa, the oldest Sanskrit thesaurus, keeps all his names.

> **CHANT** (Amarakośa, Svargavarga: names of Kubera): कुबेरस्त्र्यम्बकसखो यक्षराड्गुह्यकेश्वरः । मनुष्यधर्मा धनदो राजराजो धनाधिपः ॥ किन्नरेशो वैश्रवणः पौलस्त्यो नरवाहनः । यक्षैकपिङ्गैलविलश्रीदपुण्यजनेश्वराः ॥

- Friend of the three-eyed Śiva. King of the Yakṣas. Giver of wealth. King of kings. One-eyed, yellow-eyed. And Śrīda: the giver of Śrī, the giver of Lakṣmī.
- And it counts his treasures.

> **CHANT** (Amarakośa, nidhi verse): महापद्मश्च पद्मश्च शङ्खो मकरकच्छपौ । मुकुन्दकुन्दनीलाश्च खर्वश्च निधयो नव ॥

- Mahāpadma, Padma, Śaṅkha, Makara, Kacchapa, Mukunda, Kunda, Nīla and Kharva. Nine treasures. Nine cells. Hold on to that.

## S4 · The code inside the square  (code; Bṛhatsaṃhitā 76; Khajuraho)  ≈2:20
*[The grid, large. Lines light one by one with running totals.]*
- Now the square. Nine numbers in a row, twenty to twenty-eight, make two hundred and sixteen. Three rows share it equally: seventy-two each.
- And the centre has no choice. It must be twenty-four, one third of seventy-two. In every square like this, the middle holds a third of the whole. The centre is the point of balance.
- Now the astonishing part. Arrange the numbers one to nine in a grid: there are three hundred and sixty-two thousand, eight hundred and eighty ways.
*[A counter races to 362,880; eight survivors glow, then fold into one.]*
- Only eight are magic, and all eight are the same square, turned or flipped. In all of mathematics, there is only one.
- So Kubera's yantra is that one square, raised by nineteen. Subtract nineteen and you get eight, one, six; three, five, seven; four, nine, two: the Lo Shu of China, upside down.
- In the Navagraha yantras drawn today, the same pattern belongs to the Sun, adding to fifteen. Kubera's is that pattern lifted to seventy-two. And of a hundred and fourteen magic squares that add to seventy-two, only one is made of nine numbers in a row. Kubera's.
*[A 4×4 grid fills with drops of colour.]*
- India was building such squares fourteen centuries ago. Varāhamihira, in the Bṛhat Saṃhitā, wrote a perfume recipe on a four-by-four square where every line, even the broken diagonals, adds to eighteen.
*[REAL: the square carved at the Pārśvanātha temple, Khajuraho.]*
- And on the wall of the Pārśvanātha temple at Khajuraho, a square is carved where every row, column and diagonal, and every little block of four, adds to thirty-four.
- These were never doodles. They were recipes, and seats for the gods.

## S5 · The compass  (code; Amarakośa; Lo Shu)  ≈1:15
*[The grid lifts and turns; N, E, S, W appear.]*
- Now lay the yantra with its top toward Kubera's own direction, the North, and watch.
- The north cell holds twenty. Take away nineteen: one. And in China's map of this square, north is one: water. The south cell is twenty-eight: nine. And China's south is nine: fire. Mirror east and west, and all nine cells line up with the Chinese compass.
- One more pattern. Write the nine treasures across the numbers in the order the Amarakośa gives them. The fifth lands in the centre, on twenty-four, the heart of the square. Its name is Kacchapa: the tortoise.
*[A tortoise-shell outline settles over the centre.]*
- The square China says came on a turtle's back holds a tortoise at Kubera's heart. No scripture says this. It is simply there, hidden in plain sight.

## S6 · The breath of the yantra: mantra and rules  (Kulārṇava 6.87; TA 1.31.6; MM 16.105–111, 20.1–14; Mantra Mahārṇava; Dharmasindhu)  ≈2:40
*[The grid in a copper dish; a lamp is lit.]*
- But a yantra is not just a drawing. The Kulārṇava Tantra says:

> **CHANT** (Kulārṇava Tantra 6.87): शरीरमिव जीवस्य दीपस्य स्नेहवत्प्रिये । सर्वेषामपि देवानां तथा यन्त्रं प्रतिष्ठितम् ॥

- "As the body is to the soul, and oil to the lamp, so is the yantra for every god."
- A body needs breath. That breath is the mantra. The oldest comes from the Veda itself, and it is still sung at the end of pūjās today, in the Mantrapuṣpam.

> **CHANT** (Taittirīya Āraṇyaka 1.31.6): राजाधिराजाय प्रसह्यसाहिने । नमो वयं वैश्रवणाय कुर्महे । स मे कामान्कामकामाय मह्यम् । कामेश्वरो वैश्रवणो ददातु । कुबेराय वैश्रवणाय । महाराजाय नमः ॥

- "To the king of kings, who overcomes all by his might, to Vaiśravaṇa, we bow. May he, the lord of desires, grant my desires. To Kubera, the great king: homage."
- The japa mantra comes from the Mantramahodadhi, a manual completed in 1588.

> **CHANT** (Mantramahodadhi 16.105–107): ॐ यक्षाय कुबेराय वैश्रवणाय धनधान्याधिपतये धनधान्यसमृद्धिं मे देहि दापय स्वाहा ॥

- To the king of the Yakṣas. To Kubera, son of Viśravas. To the lord of wealth and grain. Give me abundance… and dāpaya: cause it to be given. Let it come to me through the world.
- Thirty-five syllables; with Oṃ, thirty-six, a perfect Bṛhatī. And the seer the text names for this mantra is Viśravas: Kubera's own father.
- Then his form, for meditation:

> **CHANT** (Mantramahodadhi 16.109): मनुजवाह्यविमानवरस्थितं गरुडरत्ननिभं निधिनायकम् । शिवसखं मुकुटादिविभूषितं वरगदे दधतं भज तुन्दिलम् ॥

- "Worship the round-bellied lord of treasures, seated in a chariot borne by men, shining like the garuḍa gem, friend of Śiva, crowned, giving boons and holding a mace."
*[Rule cards slide in, each with its source.]*
- And the rules. One lakh japa, with ten thousand offerings of sesame into fire. Or ten thousand japa in a Śiva temple, for increase of wealth. Or one lakh at the root of a bilva tree.
- To install the yantra: a copper dish, ghee, a bath of milk and water, a maṇḍala, and prāṇa-pratiṣṭhā, the giving of life. Where no material is named, write it on birch bark, silk or palm leaf, and seal it in gold, silver or copper.
- The Vedic rite comes at every new and full moon. The Dharmasindhu says: write Kubera's grain mantra and keep it where the grain is stored. And on Dīpāvalī night, after Lakṣmī is worshipped, Alakṣmī, misfortune, is driven from the courtyard.

## S7 · Lakṣmī's conditions, and the logic  (MBh 12.220–221; MkP 68; TA 1.31.5)  ≈2:20
*[An asura palace, lit, then dimming. Lakṣmī as a lotus of light, never a figure.]*
- But wealth is not Kubera's to create. Śrī is Lakṣmī. And in the Mahābhārata she tells Indra why she left the asuras.

> **CHANT** (Mahābhārata 12.221.26): असुरेष्ववसं पूर्वं सत्यधर्मनिबन्धना । विपरीतांस्तु तान्बुद्ध्वा त्वयि वासमरोचयम् ॥

- "I lived among the asuras, bound to truth and dharma. When I found them reversed, I chose to live with you."
- She names what she cannot live with. Houses left unswept. Sleeping past sunrise. Eating fine food alone. Grain left scattered for crows and rats. Admiring wealth that was earned wrongly.
- In another dialogue she says simply: I live in truth, in gifts, in good vows, in penance, in prowess and in virtue.
*[The grid returns. Each step lights a cell, ending at the centre.]*
- Now put it together, the way the tradition does.
- One: Lakṣmī chooses where she lives, by how people live.
- Two: the treasures serve her.
- Three: Kubera guards them. He asked to protect wealth, not to own it.
- Four: the yantra is his body, the mantra his breath, and prāṇa-pratiṣṭhā brings him in.
- Five: the square is a small cosmos. Every direction equal, at seventy-two. The centre steady. Nothing leaks.
- Six: facing north, keeping the counts, the places and the days the texts prescribe, the devotee stands in line with the guardian.
- Seven: the obstructing brother who lives in the heart is fed first. The Veda already knew where the real blocker lives.
- So this is how the tradition says the yantra works. Lakṣmī comes through worship and conduct. She stays because Kubera guards, and because we stop driving her away.

## S8 · Hidden in plain sight  (≈0:40)
*[The doorstep again. Dawn. The rice-flour grid, quiet.]*
- The oldest Kubera was a god of concealment. Treasure, in the Veda's own words, is wealth that has been put away.
- The yantra on the floor does not shout. Every line equal. The centre still. It keeps.
- Kubera never asked to be rich. He asked to guard.
- Maybe that is the code. Lakṣmī brings. Kubera keeps.
