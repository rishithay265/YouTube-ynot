Dear Editor,

I am pleased to submit the manuscript **"The Kubera Yantra: Textual Lineage, Combinatorial Structure, and an Emic Reconstruction of a 3×3 Wealth Cosmogram"** for consideration as a research article in the *Indian Journal of History of Science*.

The Kubera Yantra, a 3×3 magic square on the integers 20–28 with constant 72, is one of the most widely reproduced numerical diagrams in Indian devotional life, but almost everything written about it is unsourced. The manuscript brings together, for this one object, the textual history of Kubera and his ritual, and the history of magic squares in India. Its main contributions are:

1. **Located sources.** The widely chanted 35-syllable Kubera mantra is traced to Mahīdhara's *Mantramahodadhi* (16.105–107, dated 1588 CE), with its seer, metre, meditation verse and procedure. The *rājādhirājāya* verse of the Mantrapuṣpa is confirmed as Taittirīya Āraṇyaka 1.31.6, within the Vaiśravaṇa rite, from Mitra's 1872 edition with Sāyaṇa's commentary. The Ekapiṅgala episode is located in the Rāmāyaṇa critical text (7.13.21–30).
2. **Mathematics, checked exhaustively.** The Kubera square is shown to be the Lo Shu reflected and translated by 19, and the only one of 114 essentially distinct 3×3 magic squares with sum 72 built on consecutive integers. Varāhamihira's perfume square (*Bṛhatsaṃhitā* 76) and the Khajuraho square are re-verified as pandiagonal, and the 384 pandiagonal squares of order four are recounted. All computations are released as code.
3. **Corrections and limits.** Several frequently repeated attributions are corrected. The manuscript also states plainly what the texts do *not* contain: we found no pre-modern text giving the numeric square itself.
4. **An emic reconstruction.** The tradition's own account of the yantra's purpose is reconstructed from its premises, with every step labelled as textual fact, scholarly interpretation or hypothesis. No efficacy is claimed.

I believe the article fits the journal's scope of research on Indian science from historical perspectives, and continues work published in its pages, notably the 2021 study of Varāhamihira's perfume combinatorics.

An earlier version of this manuscript is available as a preprint on Zenodo (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24540072), where the code and full computational output are also deposited. The manuscript is not under consideration elsewhere, has not been published in a peer-reviewed venue, and I have no competing interests. As required by the journal's policy, the use of AI-based tools is documented in the Sources and Method section; I take full responsibility for the content.
