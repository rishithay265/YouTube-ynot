### Description (paste as is)
The Kubera Yantra is a 3×3 array of the integers 20–28 in which every row, column and main diagonal sums to 72; in South India it is also drawn in rice flour as the kubera kolam. Popular literature repeats a small set of claims about it, mostly unsourced. This paper does three things. First, it traces the deity and the ritual apparatus attached to the yantra through primary texts read in critical or standard editions: the Atharvaveda (8.10.28), the Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa (13.4.3.10), the Taittirīya Āraṇyaka (1.31, the Vaiśravaṇa rite whose sixth section supplies the rājādhirājāya verse of the Mantrapuṣpa), the Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa (7.3, 7.11, 7.13, 7.15), the Mahābhārata (12.221), the Amarakośa, the Mārkaṇḍeya and Matsya Purāṇas, the Kulārṇava Tantra, the Mantramahodadhi (1588 CE) and the Dharmasindhu. Among other results, we locate the widely chanted 35-syllable Kubera mantra and its meditation verse in Mantramahodadhi 16.105–109, and the "Ekapiṅgala" story in Rāmāyaṇa 7.13.21–30. Second, it proves and machine-checks the mathematics: the Kubera square is the Lo Shu reflected top-to-bottom and translated by 19; it is the only 3×3 magic square on nine consecutive integers with sum 72 among 114 essentially different squares with distinct positive entries and that sum; Varāhamihira's perfume square (Bṛhatsaṃhitā 76.23–25) and the Khajuraho square are pandiagonal, and we recount the 384 (48 up to symmetry) pandiagonal squares of order four, all of which are most-perfect. Third, it maps the grid onto the eight quarters under five drawing conventions and offers an explicitly labelled emic reconstruction of how the tradition itself explains the yantra's purpose. Every statement is tagged as textual fact, established scholarly interpretation, or new hypothesis. No causal efficacy is claimed. Code and full computational output are included in this record.
